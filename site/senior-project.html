The problem

Customer: NASA · Users: Artemis astronauts

The environmental control and life support system (ECLSS) keeps astronauts alive, and it is loud. On a small lander, that noise hurts speech clarity and adds a steady stress load, and low frequencies in particular affect concentration and sleep.

NASA crew acoustics guidance does not allow hearing protection as the way to comply, so the noise has to be controlled in the hardware. Our brief: suppress ECLSS noise inside the capsule without changing how the ECLSS works, and fit inside the lander.

Missions run about 30 days at the Moon and up to 1,200 days at Mars, so the design also has to be durable and repairable by the crew.

  • Noise suppression< 70 dBA
  • Target frequency band60–200 Hz
  • Volumeabout 1 m³
  • Launch loadsup to 8 g, 1.5× margin
  • Durability5+ missions
  • Room sizes it must adapt to60 to 1,000 m³

How the design changed

Concept 1 · Preliminary design review

Hinged foldable panels

Rhombus tiles hinged on every side, filled with damping foam. Fold the panels one way for high frequencies and another way for low ones. It scored highest in a weighted decision matrix across 11 criteria, ahead of hex-shell and linear-folding panels, mostly on adjustability.

The PDR already flagged two concerns: many actuators means many electrical failure points, and repeated folding wears the hinges.

Concept 2 · Critical to final design review

Tunable membrane absorber

A membrane over a sealed air cavity acts like a mass on a spring. Changing the cavity depth tunes it to the dominant noise frequency. One moving axis replaces a lattice of folding panels, and the actuators sit outside the cavity where they can be replaced.

It also targets the 60–200 Hz band that thin foam panels handle poorly.

The final design

Three subsystems

The membrane and the sealed air behind it form an acoustic spring. When sound at the right frequency hits the membrane, it resonates and dissipates the energy. A deeper cavity tunes the absorber lower, a shallower one tunes it higher.

A microphone finds the loudest frequency in the room, and the controller moves a mounting plate to the matching depth. The controller is a finite state machine: initialize, sample, identify frequency, calculate depth, adjust, hold, and fault handling.

f₀ = 1/(2π) · √( γP₀ / (mₛ · d) )

γ is the ratio of specific heats of air, P₀ is ambient pressure, mₛ is the membrane's mass per area, and d is the cavity depth.

Membrane cartridge

Dense fluorosilicone membrane, top and bottom plates, gasket, and compression screws. 3 mm material covers 60–170 Hz and 2 mm covers 76–200 Hz, so a mix of the two spans the full band.

Reverberation chamber

A fluorosilicone accordion housing, thinner at the folds so it bends cleanly. A 13 mm orifice in the base plate lets cavity pressure equalize while the depth changes.

Control system

Frequency sensor, controller, two linear actuators, and four linear rails. Feedforward control maps frequency to depth. Closed-loop feedback and FFT averaging are the planned upgrades.

Verification

Feasibility prototype · May 2026
Actuator and electronics
60–250Hz

Frequency sweep. The controller smoothed noisy readings, and the actuators moved smoothly in both directions across the full height range.

Pressure equalization
4.49s

Average over five tests, each shifting the chamber depth three inches. The prototype used a small seal gap. The flight design uses a 13 mm orifice.

Launch vibration
10.3g RMS

Random profile from 20 to 2000 Hz on a shake table, ramped through 25, 50, and 75 percent, then held at 100 percent for 5 minutes. The only issue was loose hex nuts, so we switched to lock nuts.

What these tests did and did not show. They verified that the mechanism tunes, equalizes pressure, and survives launch-like vibration. The prototype used a TPU housing and mass-loaded vinyl membrane instead of flight materials. The planned acoustic test, measuring low-frequency noise reduction against the NC-40 criterion, was not completed because we did not have a suitable test facility. The predicted noise reduction comes from analysis, not measurement.

Path to flight

Risks and mitigations

  • Pressure differential, the top risk. An unequalized cavity detunes the absorber, so the base orifice is sized and tested.
  • Electrical failure. Space-rated wiring, strain relief, current limits, and a fail-safe default depth.
  • Positioning and sensing errors. Position feedback, travel limits, and recurring microphone calibration.
  • Mass and volume. Lightweight materials and a 0.105 m shorter maximum depth.

Next steps

  • Full-scale system. Multiple panels, each with its own sensor, to cover separate noise sources.
  • Flight-material retest. Repeat the vibration test, then inspect for loosening, membrane damage, and seal degradation.
  • Better control. Closed-loop feedback, FFT averaging, and a deadband to prevent constant motion.
  • Lifecycle budget. About $5.63 million to develop one flight-ready unit, with a 10-person team over three years and a 30% margin.

My role

Absorber System Design Lead

I led the analytical modeling and design of the tunable membrane absorber, and worked on the sensor-integrated actuator and its testing. On the team's operations side, I kept the file organization and meeting notes.

  • Modeled the absorber to hit 60–200 Hz and cut the maximum cavity depth from 0.305 m to 0.205 m while keeping the target frequency coverage.
  • Designed and validated the actuator that swept 60–250 Hz, equalized pressure in 4.49 s, and survived simulated launch vibration at 10.3 g RMS.
  • Co-authored and presented the NASA technical paper.
